# LF002 The Insane Plan to Drain the Mediterranean Sea (Atlantropa)

One dam.

That was the whole plan. One wall of concrete across the Strait of Gibraltar, and the Mediterranean Sea would begin to disappear.

Its water would sink by up to two hundred metres. New land, bigger than France, would rise out of the sea. And Europe and Africa would be joined into a single new continent.

It sounds like science fiction. But for twenty-five years, one man worked on it as if it were real. He drew the maps. He wrote the books. He convinced engineers and newspapers that it could actually be done.

This is the story of Atlantropa. The greatest project that was never built.

His name was Herman Sörgel, an architect from Munich.

In the years after the First World War, Europe was broken. Cities had been destroyed, economies had collapsed, and people feared that another war would come.

Sörgel had an unusual answer. He believed Europe did not need another treaty. It needed one gigantic project. A project so big that every country would have to work together to build it.

And he had a real example in front of him. Just next door, the Dutch were building a huge dam to close off an inland sea, the Zuiderzee, and turn its floor into new land. If the Netherlands could push back the sea, Sörgel thought, why not do the same with the Mediterranean?

In 1927, he started drawing. At first he called it Panropa. Later, he gave it the name that would follow him for the rest of his life. Atlantropa.

The heart of the plan was the dam.

It would stretch around thirty-five kilometres across the Strait of Gibraltar, curving between Spain and Morocco. It would stand about three hundred metres high. Taller than any dam on Earth at the time.

Some experts doubted there was even enough concrete in the world to build it.

And it would not be built in a few years. Sörgel imagined a project lasting more than a century, passed from one generation of engineers to the next. A cathedral of concrete for the whole of Europe.

But the dam was not only a wall. It was a machine. Water from the Atlantic would be allowed to pour back in through enormous turbines, turning the dam into one of the largest power stations ever imagined. Electricity for an entire continent.

Here is the strange part. Sörgel did not need pumps to drain the Mediterranean.

The sun would do it for him.

Every year, the Mediterranean loses more water to evaporation than its rivers can give back. The only thing keeping it full is the Atlantic Ocean, pouring in through Gibraltar. Close that door, and the sea would slowly start to sink.

Sörgel planned more dams to control it. One between Sicily and Tunisia would split the sea in two. Another at the Dardanelles would hold back the Black Sea.

The western basin would drop by about one hundred metres. The eastern basin, by about two hundred.

And here is something Sörgel could not have fully known. The Mediterranean has dried up before.

Around six million years ago, long before humans, movements of the Earth closed the connection to the Atlantic. Cut off from the ocean, the sea evaporated. Scientists call it the Messinian salinity crisis. Much of the basin became a deep, scorching desert of salt, in places kilometres below today's sea level.

Then, about five and a half million years ago, the Atlantic broke back in through Gibraltar. Some researchers believe the water came back in an unimaginable flood, refilling the whole sea in as little as a few years.

Nature had already run Sörgel's experiment. And then it reversed it.

And then, the land would appear.

Around six hundred thousand square kilometres of new ground. More than the whole of France. Space for farms, for new cities, for millions of people.

A road and a railway would run from Europe, across Sicily, all the way to Africa. Two continents, joined right through the middle of the sea that had always kept them apart.

And that was the real dream. Not just a dam, but a new continent. Atlantropa.

But the dream had a dark side.

Great port cities would have been left stranded, far from the water they were built on. The climate of the whole region could have changed in ways no one understood.

And in Africa, Sörgel even imagined damming the Congo River to create an enormous inland sea. He wrote about Africa as a territory that would be useful to Europe. The millions of people who already lived there hardly appeared in his plans at all.

Atlantropa was a dream of peace for Europe. For much of the rest of the world, it would have been something very different.

For a while, people took it seriously. Sörgel wrote four books about Atlantropa and around a thousand articles and publications. Engineers debated it. Newspapers printed his maps. Even famous architects spoke in support of it.

Today, the Deutsches Museum in Munich still keeps an Atlantropa archive, full of drawings, letters of support and hundreds of press articles. Proof of just how close the idea came to being taken as a real plan.

But Germany's new leaders in the 1930s had no interest in his peaceful continent. Then came the Second World War.

After the war, Sörgel tried again. In 1950, a German film even borrowed from his ideas. But the world was changing. New energy sources were coming, and a sea-sized power plant suddenly seemed less necessary.

On Christmas Day, 1952, Herman Sörgel was killed in a road accident. He had spent twenty-five years on a single idea.

Eight years later, the Atlantropa Institute was quietly dissolved.

Today, the Strait of Gibraltar is still open. Ships pass through it every day, and the Atlantic still pours quietly into the Mediterranean, just as it always has.

But somewhere in an archive, the drawings are still there. The new coastlines. The bridges. The dam.

So tell me. If it were possible today, would you build it?

Because it all started with just one idea. One dam.
