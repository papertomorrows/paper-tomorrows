# EP005 (EN) You Breathe 20,000 Times a Day. You're Probably Doing It Wrong | Breathing for Your Nervous System, Sleep and Spirit

You take about twenty thousand breaths a day.

And most of them, you're probably taking the wrong way. Too fast. Too shallow. Through your mouth.

If your heart races for no reason, if sleep won't come at night, if rest never quite feels like rest, the place it starts might just be your breath.

Here's the fascinating part. Breathing is the only automatic function in your body you can change at will. You can't slow your heartbeat on command. But your breath? You can change it right now. And when you change your breath, your body, your mind, your sleep, your days, and even your spirit change with it.

That's why almost every spiritual tradition in history has called the breath the first doorway to awakening.

Today, I'll walk you through the breathing that science has shown really works, the many ways the world's spiritual traditions have used the breath, and how your breath is woven into your sleep, your life and your spiritual growth. Stay to the end, and we'll practice one technique together that you can use tonight.

First, why does the breath have so much power?

Your body runs on an autonomic nervous system. One side, the sympathetic system, gets you ready for action. The other, the parasympathetic system, helps you rest and recover. Like a seesaw, the two keep balancing each other, quietly running your heart, your digestion, your temperature and your sleep without you ever thinking about it.

And here's the key. When you breathe in, your heart speeds up slightly. When you breathe out, it slows down slightly. So the longer your exhale, the more your body feels safe, and shifts toward rest.

That's why anxiety makes your breath short and fast. And it's why a long, slow exhale can quiet your mind.

<!-- section: autonomic | visuals: city night traffic, person phone night, tired office worker, crowded subway, rain window, calm forest path | title: A Nervous System That Never Switches Off | ai: a tired person sitting alone at night in a small apartment lit only by a phone screen, faint tangled threads of red light around their chest slowly loosening into soft blue light as they breathe -->
But today, for a lot of people, that seesaw is stuck on one side.

Notifications from morning to night. Work that never really ends. Screens right up until the moment we close our eyes. Your body might be sitting still, but your nervous system is on alert all day long.

And then the signals show up. A racing heart for no clear reason. A stomach that won't settle. Cold hands and feet. A heavy head. Exhausted, but unable to sleep. The doctor says nothing is wrong, yet your body keeps struggling. This is often described as autonomic imbalance, or in its more serious medical forms, dysautonomia.

The autonomic nervous system, as the name says, doesn't take orders. But there is one dial you can reach directly. And that dial is your breath.

A 2017 meta-analysis pooled studies of heart rate variability biofeedback, a way of training slow, steady breathing, and found that people who practiced it reported large reductions in stress and anxiety. And study after study shows that simply breathing slowly increases the activity of the vagus nerve, the main nerve that tells your body to rest.

One important note. If a racing heart, dizziness or digestive problems keep coming back, please see a doctor first to rule out other causes. Breathing isn't a replacement for treatment. It's the closest tool you have for caring for your body every single day.

<!-- section: basics | visuals: nose breathing, yoga breathing, calm breathing woman, meditation morning, slow motion water | title: The Three Basics of Good Breathing -->
So what does good breathing look like? Just three basics.

One. Breathe through your nose. Your nose isn't just a tube. It filters, warms, and humidifies the air. In the 1990s, researchers discovered that the sinuses around your nose produce nitric oxide, a molecule that helps widen blood vessels. Breathe through your nose, and you breathe it in too.

Two. Breathe with your belly. If your shoulders and chest rise every time you breathe, you're breathing shallowly. Let your belly gently expand as you inhale, and fall as you exhale. That's your diaphragm doing its job.

Three. Breathe slowly. Most adults breathe twelve to twenty times a minute. But researchers found that something special happens at about six breaths a minute, around ten seconds per breath. Your heart rhythm begins to rise and fall in a big, smooth wave with your breath, strengthening your body's ability to balance itself. It's called resonance breathing. For a tired nervous system, it might be the best gift you can give.

<!-- section: science | visuals: scientist lab, breathing exercise, sigh relief, calm office, sunrise window | title: Three Techniques Science Is Watching | ai: a glowing human silhouette sitting in meditation with soft waves of blue and gold light expanding from the chest in calm rings >> rings of light expand slowly from the chest and fade, the figure breathes gently, particles float -->
Now, three techniques scientists have paid special attention to.

The first is the physiological sigh. Take a big breath in through your nose, then a second short inhale on top to fully fill your lungs, then a long, slow exhale through your mouth. In 2023, a Stanford team tested this with 108 people, five minutes a day for a month, comparing several breathing practices and meditation. Cyclic sighing improved mood the most.

The second is box breathing. Breathe in for four, hold for four, breathe out for four, hold for four. Like tracing the four sides of a box. It's known as a method used by elite military units to stay calm under extreme pressure.

The third is four-seven-eight breathing. In through your nose for four, hold for seven, and out slowly through your mouth for eight. It was popularized by the American physician Andrew Weil, and with that long exhale, it's especially good before sleep.

<!-- section: sleep | visuals: night bedroom, moon night, sleeping woman, stars night sky, soft lamp night, rain window night | title: Change Your Breath, Change Your Sleep | ai: a person sleeping peacefully in a quiet bedroom lit by soft moonlight, a gentle silver blue glow rising and falling over their chest, stars visible through the window >> the silver glow over the chest rises and falls slowly like calm breathing, curtains sway gently, stars twinkle softly -->
And one of the biggest things your breath can change is your sleep.

You can't force sleep. It only arrives when your body feels safe and your nervous system shifts toward rest. But if your mind is still full of the day, your body may be lying down while your nerves are wide awake.

That's why your breath before bed matters so much. In studies of people with insomnia, those who practiced slow breathing before sleep showed more activity in the nerves that help the body rest, and reported better sleep quality.

It's simple. Lights off, lie down, and do four rounds of four-seven-eight breathing. Or breathe about six times a minute, with the exhale a little longer than the inhale. If you lose count, that just means sleep is on its way.

The same goes for waking up in the middle of the night. Don't check the clock. Don't pick up your phone. Just count your breaths, in and out, one, two. Instead of fighting your thoughts, come back to your breath.

Your breath during sleep matters too. Sleeping with your mouth open dries your mouth, makes snoring more likely, and makes deep rest harder. Practicing nose breathing during the day slowly changes how you breathe at night. But if you snore heavily, or someone tells you that you stop breathing in your sleep, please get a sleep test.

<!-- section: life | visuals: family dinner, friends talking, person studying, woman walking city, couple walking park, office meeting calm | title: Your Breath Touches Everything in Your Life -->
In truth, your breath isn't only connected to sleep. It's connected to every moment of your life.

Think about the last time you got angry. Your breath stopped, or turned rough. When you're startled, you gasp. When you're relieved, you sigh. Emotions change your breath first. But it works the other way, too. Change your breath, and your emotions follow.

Focus works the same way. When you study or work, you often hold your breath without noticing. And the shallower your breath, the faster your mind tires. A single long exhale once an hour can clear your head.

So does digestion. Your body digests well only when the resting system is switched on. Before you eat in a hurry, take three slow breaths.

Posture is tied to your breath, too. Hunch over a screen for hours, and your chest gets squeezed and your breath gets shallow. Simply opening your shoulders deepens your breath.

And so are your relationships. When you want to snap back at someone, take one breath first. That one breath turns a reaction into a response. It's the shortest pause there is, and it can save you from words you'd regret.

So the way you breathe is, in the end, the way you live.

<!-- section: spirit | visuals: ancient temple, candle light, monks meditation, incense smoke, mountain temple | title: Breath Once Meant Spirit | ai: an ancient stone temple at dawn with soft incense smoke rising into golden light, ancient scripts of many cultures faintly glowing in the air >> incense smoke curls slowly upward through the beams of light, the glowing symbols drift and fade gently -->
And here's something truly mysterious.

The English word spirit comes from the Latin spiritus. And its original meaning? Breath.

The Greek pneuma, the Hebrew ruach, the Sanskrit prana. All of them mean breath or wind, and also spirit or life force.

Civilizations far apart from one another all used the same word for breath and for spirit. To the ancients, breath was life itself, living inside us.

<!-- section: traditions | visuals: yoga meditation, buddhist monk, tai chi morning, prayer hands, forest meditation, lotus | title: Breathing in the Spiritual Traditions | ai: four figures meditating in a circle on a quiet hilltop at sunrise, each in a different tradition's simple clothing, a single soft flame of light glowing in the center >> the central flame breathes softly brighter and dimmer, the sunrise light grows, grass moves in the breeze -->
So the world's spiritual traditions each developed their own ways of breathing.

In Indian yoga, there's pranayama, the practice of guiding prana, the life force. One form, nadi shodhana or alternate nostril breathing, means closing your right nostril with your thumb and breathing in through the left, then switching and breathing out through the right. It's said to balance body and mind. There's also ujjayi, a gentle narrowing at the back of the throat that makes the breath sound like the ocean.

In Buddhism, there's anapanasati, mindfulness of breathing in and out. Tradition says the Buddha practiced it on the night of his awakening. You don't try to change the breath. You simply watch it come and go.

In Taoist and Korean traditions, there's danjeon breathing. You rest your attention just below the navel, and breathe deep, quiet breaths into the lower belly, gathering energy there.

And in Eastern Christianity, monks have long repeated a short prayer in rhythm with the breath. One phrase on the inhale, one on the exhale, until the breath itself becomes prayer.

The traditions differ, but the teaching is remarkably the same. The moment you notice your breath, you return to here and now.

<!-- section: growth | visuals: mountain path sunrise, staircase light, forest light rays, sunrise clouds, tree roots, milky way | title: Five Steps of Spiritual Growth Through the Breath | ai: a winding stone path climbing a gentle mountain toward a radiant sunrise, five softly glowing stepping stones along the way, a lone figure walking upward in peaceful light -->
So how does the breath actually help us grow spiritually? I like to describe it as five steps.

The first step is noticing your body. Are your shoulders raised right now? Is your belly moving? Through the breath, you come home to your body, maybe for the first time.

The second step is guiding your emotions. When anger or anxiety rises, instead of being swept away, you breathe out slowly. The feeling is still there, but it no longer drags you around.

The third step is staying here and now. Your breath only ever exists in the present. You can't breathe yesterday's breath, or tomorrow's. Follow it, and a quiet gap opens up between one thought and the next.

The fourth step is meeting the one who watches. As you observe your breath, you suddenly realize something. There is a body that breathes, and there is you, watching that breath. Many traditions call this watching awareness your true self.

And the fifth step is feeling that everything is connected. The breath you just took in was breathed out by trees and oceans somewhere. And the breath you just let go, a tree will breathe in again. You have never, not for a single moment, breathed alone. Through the breath, you and the world are one.

<!-- section: caution | visuals: ice water, cold lake, dizzy, ocean storm, sunset calm | title: Be Careful with Intense Breathwork -->
But there's something important you need to know.

Some popular breathing methods today involve fast, forceful breathing. The best known is the method of Wim Hof, the Iceman. In a 2014 study, people trained in his technique showed some ability to influence their body's responses.

But intense breathwork can cause dizziness and even fainting. Never do it in water or while driving. And if you have a heart condition, are pregnant, or experience panic attacks, please talk to a professional first. And if your nervous system is already worn out, start with slow, gentle breathing rather than intense breathwork.

Spiritual growth doesn't come from rushing or pushing your body. Gentle, steady breath takes you deepest.

<!-- section: routine | visuals: sunrise bedroom, coffee morning calm, office window, night bedroom, moon night | title: Your Daily Breathing Routine -->
So here's what to try, starting today.

In the morning, five minutes of the physiological sigh. It helps you start the day lighter and brighter.

During tense moments in the day, just four rounds of box breathing. Perfect before a meeting or an important call. And once an hour, open your shoulders and take one long exhale.

At night, put your phone down and use four-seven-eight breathing, or resonance breathing at six breaths a minute, to let your body rest. If you wake up in the night, count your breaths instead of checking the clock.

And at any moment in the day, just notice your breath three times. How am I breathing right now? That one question is the beginning of spiritual practice.
